Srinagar's water is staying,
but less of it stays year-round

How have the areas of permanent and seasonal surface water inside Srinagar's urban boundary (64.9 km²) changed from 1990 to 2021? A 32-year, 30 m Landsat record, mapped and measured.

Explore the mapRead the findings

Key findings

Comparing three-year means at each end (1991–93 vs 2019–21) so one unusual year cannot drive the result.

Permanent water
−28.6%

5.37 → 3.83 km². A steady decline of 0.061 km² per year (p < 0.001).

Seasonal water
+44.3%

4.56 → 6.58 km². Peaked in 2016 at 7.04 km², the year permanent water hit its low.

Total water
≈ 10 km²

9.92 → 10.41 km² (+4.9%). Roughly 16% of the city, with little net change.

Seasonal share of water
45% → 63%

The change is in persistence, not quantity: water is present, but less of it all year.

Conclusion. Srinagar's surface water stayed close to 10 km², but a growing share of it is seasonal rather than permanent. The rise in seasonal water is concentrated in the last decade: period means were 4.77 and 4.79 km² in 1991–2000 and 2001–10, then 6.07 km² in 2011–21.

Data and method

Dataset

SourceJRC Global Surface Water v1.4
JRC/GSW1_4/YearlyHistory
BandwaterClass: 0 no data, 1 not water, 2 seasonal, 3 permanent
Resolution30 m, one image per year, 1990–2021
Study areaSrinagar Municipal Corporation (J&K), 64.9 km², from Indian Urban Local Body boundaries
PlatformGoogle Earth Engine, geemap, Python

Workflow

  • Filter the collection to 1990–2021 and clip to the city boundary.
  • Apply one fixed visualisation to every year.
  • Sum pixelArea per class with reduceRegion at 30 m.
  • Summarise with three-year end means and regression slopes.
  • Export frames, a labelled GIF and a transition map for this site.

Limitations

  • Not depth or volume. The classes describe water occurrence, not water level or quality.
  • Pixel size. At 30 m, narrow channels and small ponds are missed.
  • Missing data. No-data covered 13.6% of the city in 1990, 4.8% in 1995 and 16.1% in 1997, so those years are excluded and the baseline starts in 1991.
  • Annual classes. Short floods and dry spells fold into one label, and the number of valid observations in a year can shift the class.
  • Cause. This dataset cannot explain why seasonal water increased. Urbanisation or climate would need further evidence.
